Verifying a printed paper: what the QR code is today, and what it should become

Built (2026-10-11), phase 1. You approved the plan with all seven recommendations. Prescriptions (dental and consultation) now carry a QR that opens the clinic's own record, with a short code under it. The page shows Genuine, Cancelled, A newer version exists, Past its validity or Already dispensed; the doctor's name follows the "Doctor's name" switch; the clinic can set how many days a prescription stays valid in Settings › Printing › Prescriptions. Not yet: certificates, lab reports and receipts; outside pharmacies marking a paper used; doctors' licence numbers; Amharic on the check page.

You asked what the QR code on a prescription is for, what a scan returns, and how it could prove a paper is genuine. This page answers that plainly, shows what I propose with a day in the life of each case, and ends with the decisions I need from you. Phase 1 of this plan is now built (see above).

1. The honest answer: today it is a label, not a check

The QR on a prescription proves nothing today. It holds a few lines of plain text. No server looks at it, and anyone who can type the same lines into a free QR generator can print an identical one.
PaperWhat is inside the QR today (plain text, as printed)

The short text under the QR (for example Rx 7gbzsm) is the last six characters of the prescription's id. When a phone scans it, it just shows those lines. It does not say whether the prescription is real, cancelled, altered or already used. It also carries the patient's name and card number in clear text, which is printed on the paper anyway.

2. What you proposed, and one adjustment

Your idea: put everything in the QR (patient, doctor, drugs, date) plus a unique id, so scanning tells you whether it is correct.
My suggestion: keep the QR small and make it a key, not a copy. The QR holds only an unguessable link. The clinic's own record, on the clinic's server, holds the truth. Scanning opens a page that shows that record.

Why not put everything in the QR? Three reasons that matter for a pharmacy:

  • A QR full of data cannot be switched off. If the doctor cancels a prescription, a printed QR cannot change. A link to the record can say "Cancelled".
  • It cannot say "already used". A copy of a genuine prescription carries identical data; only a server that remembers can notice the second use.
  • It makes the paper a bigger privacy leak and harder to scan. More data means a denser code, which a thermal printer and a cheap phone camera read badly.

Your worry, "people may use the exact same QR code for a fake prescription", is the right worry. The link design answers it: the scan shows the original content from the clinic's record, so a paper whose drugs or doses were changed no longer matches the screen.

3. A day in the life: what the pharmacist sees

A. A genuine prescription. Abebe takes his prescription to a pharmacy. Selam, the pharmacist, scans the QR. A green page opens: "Genuine · issued by Cure Dental Clinic, 11 Oct 2026", then the medicines. She compares the screen with the paper. They match. She dispenses.
B. A changed paper. Someone altered "7 days" to "70 days" on the paper. The QR still opens the genuine record, which says 7 days. Selam sees the difference between paper and screen. The paper is refused.
C. A photocopy used twice. A genuine prescription is photocopied and taken to a second pharmacy. The page now says "Already checked 2 times; first on 11 Oct at 10:40". If the clinic's own pharmacy dispensed it, it says "Dispensed on 11 Oct". Not proof of fraud, but a clear reason to ask.
D. A made-up paper. A forger prints a QR that points nowhere, or at a lookalike. The scan says "Not found" (or lands on a different website, which is why the pharmacist is taught to look for the clinic system's address).
E. A cancelled prescription. Dr Yonas cancels it the same afternoon. The page turns red: "Cancelled by the clinic".
F. No internet at the pharmacy. The page cannot load. The paper still carries the reference and a short code. The pharmacy can check later or call the clinic. We never print anything that makes a paper unusable without a connection.
G. A shared doctor account. The clinic switched "Doctor's name" off. The verification page then shows the clinic, not a doctor's name, so it matches what the paper says.

6. How it would work

  1. First print: the system asks the server for a verification token for that prescription. The server writes down a snapshot of what is being printed, built from its own records and not from the browser, and returns a long random token and a short code.
  2. The QR becomes the link app.clinicos.cc/v/<token>. Under it, the reference and a short code.
  3. A reprint uses the same token and is already marked DUPLICATE. It is a copy of the same record, not a new one.
  4. A scan opens a public page, with no sign-in. It shows the snapshot, its status (genuine, cancelled, newer version exists), and how many times it was checked.
  5. If the prescription is cancelled or edited after printing, the page says so at once; a newer print gets a new token.
  6. When our own pharmacy dispenses it, the record is marked dispensed, so a second use shows "Dispensed".

Safety measures

  • The token is 128 bits of randomness, so it cannot be guessed. Only a hash of it is stored.
  • Lookups are rate-limited. An unknown token and a wrong token give the same answer, so nobody can probe which exist.
  • The public page is excluded from search engines and carries no sign-in, no patient phone or card number.
  • The public lookup works across clinics through one narrow database function, so the usual clinic isolation is not loosened elsewhere.
  • Typing by hand (when a QR will not scan) needs the reference and the short code, and is rate-limited too.

8. What this cannot do

  • A perfect photocopy of a genuine paper still looks genuine on its own. Only the clinic's memory (used, scanned twice) tells the second one apart. An outside pharmacy cannot mark it used until phase 3.
  • A pharmacist who does not scan, or does not compare the screen with the paper, is not protected. The page is a tool; the habit is the control.
  • A QR that points to a lookalike website would show a fake "genuine" page. We reduce it by using one fixed address and by training pharmacists to look at it.
  • It proves the paper matches what the clinic printed, not that the treatment was right. It is a check on the paper, not on the medicine.

9. What I need from you

  1. QR as a link to the clinic's record (option A), rather than all the data inside? I recommend yes.
  2. How much of the patient to show on the public page. Recommended: first name and initial, age and sex. Alternatives: full name; or nothing.
  3. The doctor's name and licence on the page follow the "Doctor's name" switch of that paper. Recommended yes.
  4. Show "already checked N times" to the next person who scans. Recommended yes; it shows times only, never who.
  5. Prescription validity. Show "issued N days ago" always; and optionally let each clinic set "valid for N days" that turns the page amber after that. Recommended: both.
  6. Papers in the first release. Recommended: prescriptions only, then certificates as the next small step.
  7. When a prescription is edited after printing, the old paper says "A newer version exists. Ask the clinic". Recommended yes.
