The Pharmacy, in plain language

From switching on an empty pharmacy and registering your stock, to a normal working day — for a small clinic pharmacy and a large one.

First: two sizes of pharmacy, same software

The pharmacy has 10 pages. You don't need all of them all the time — which ones matter depends on how big your pharmacy is. The software ships with two pharmacy roles:

🏠 Small clinic pharmacy

One person — the pharmacist — does everything: dispensing, selling, ordering, counting.

They hold the manager role, so every page is open to them. Most days they live on just 2–3 pages and visit the rest weekly or monthly.

🏥 Large clinic pharmacy

A team with split jobs: a manager, several dispensers, a stock-keeper, and a cashier at the front desk.

Each person sees only the pages their job needs. The manager runs the “back office”; the floor staff run the counter.

Throughout this guide: ETB = Birr. “FEFO” = First-Expiry-First-Out — always sell the lot that expires soonest, so nothing dies on the shelf.

Starting from zero: setting up a brand-new pharmacy

The clinic just installed the software. Sara logs into the pharmacy for the very first time and every page is empty — no drugs, no stock, no suppliers. The software doesn't know a single thing about your pharmacy yet. Before she can serve one patient, she has to teach it what she has. Here's the order she does it in.

If you don't see the Pharmacy section in the sidebar, that's the first call to make. (Setting up which staff can do what — dispensing vs purchasing vs counting — also happens there.)

Step 1 — Build your drug list · Catalog admin

This is the foundation, and the very first thing. Until a drug exists in your catalog, you cannot stock it, dispense it, or sell it. On Catalog admin, add each product you carry, one at a time: its name, form (tablet, syrup, injection…), unit price in ETB, a reorder level (the stock level at which the system warns you “low stock”), whether it's a controlled drug, and that it's active.

💡 Naming tip that saves you pain later.

Spell each drug the way your doctors will write it on prescriptions. When a prescription comes in, the system matches it to your stock by name. If the doctor writes “Amoxicillin 500mg” and your catalog says “Amoxicillin caps,” the line won't match and it'll tell the patient to buy outside. Agree on names with the clinical team up front.

Picture it Sara spends her first morning with her shelf list open, typing in her ~150 products and their prices. Tedious once; then it's done.
⚠ Heads-up Two real limits bite right here. (1) No bulk import — you add drugs one at a time; there's no spreadsheet/CSV upload, so a big formulary is a long afternoon. (2) This catalog is separate from the doctors' prescribing list — they're linked only by exact drug name, so if the names don't match, prescriptions won't find your stock. Both are in the limitations list at the end.

Step 2 — Add your suppliers · Purchasing

On the Purchasing page, add the wholesalers and distributors you buy from. You only need their basic details. You'll attach every purchase to one of these suppliers, which later powers the supplier-performance report.

Picture it Sara adds her two regular distributors. That's it for now.

Step 3 — Load your opening stock · Batches & expiry, or Purchasing

Your drugs now exist, but every one shows 0 on hand — the software doesn't know what's on your shelves. There are two ways to put stock in, and which you use depends on the situation:

  • Stock is already on your shelves (an opening balance): go to Batches & expiry and add a batch for each drug — its batch number, expiry date, and quantity. This is the quick, direct way to enter what you physically already have.
  • You're recording a real delivery from a supplier: use Purchasing — create a purchase order for what's arriving, then click Receive. This creates the batches and records what you paid. This is the normal way you'll add stock from now on.
Picture it Sara's shelves were filled by the clinic's opening order, so she enters each lot directly on the Batches & expiry page with its expiry and count. Next month, when her first supplier delivery arrives, she'll use the Receive button in Purchasing instead.
⚠ Heads-up If you load opening stock through a purchase order, each line's cost pre-fills with the selling price, not what you actually paid — leave that and your cost/margin/valuation reports will be wrong. Either overwrite the real cost on every line, or (simpler for an opening balance) add batches directly on the Batches page. And as in Step 1, there's no bulk import for stock either — it's batch by batch.

Step 4 — Check that it's right · Drug catalog + Dashboard

Open the Drug catalog: every drug should now show the correct quantity on hand. Open the Pharmacy dashboard: it should show your total inventory value (ETB) and flag anything that's already low or near expiry. If the numbers match your shelves, your pharmacy is live.

Picture it Sara cross-checks a few shelves against the Drug catalog, sees the Dashboard value looks right, and switches the “open” sign on. From here, the day-to-day below takes over.

In a large pharmacy the same four steps are split by role: the manager builds the drug list and adds suppliers (steps 1–2), the stock-keeper loads the opening stock and batches (step 3), and the manager confirms it on the Dashboard (step 4). Same spine — define → stock → sell — just more hands.

A day at a small clinic pharmacy

Meet Sara, the only pharmacist at a small clinic in Addis. Here is her whole day, and which pages she touches.

Morning · opening up Sara opens the Pharmacy dashboard with her coffee. It tells her at a glance: 3 drugs are low on stock, 2 lots are near expiry, and there are no open orders. She makes a mental note to reorder later.
A patient arrives from the doctor Dr. Bekele finishes a consultation with Almaz and signs her prescription. It appears automatically in Sara's Dispense queue — she didn't have to do anything. She sees “R-1042 · Almaz · 2 items” with a small allergy check flag (Almaz has a penicillin allergy on file).

Sara clicks the row to open the Dispense detail page. It lists the two drugs. One is Amoxicillin — and because of the penicillin allergy, the page shows a red warning. Sara phones the doctor, who switches it. For each drug Sara picks the stock lot (the page suggests the one expiring soonest) and the quantity, then clicks Prepare. This prices the prescription and sends the bill to the front desk.

Almaz pays at reception. The moment the payment lands, Sara clicks Complete — the system subtracts the pills from stock and Sara hands over the medicine with counselling. Done.
Midday · a walk-in A man walks in with no prescription, just wanting Paracetamol for a headache. Sara opens the OTC sale page, searches “Paracetamol”, adds 1 pack to the cart, takes 35 ETB cash, and the sale is done in 10 seconds. Stock drops automatically.
Afternoon · restocking Remembering the low-stock warning, Sara opens Purchasing. She picks her supplier, makes a quick order (a “PO”) for more Amoxicillin and Paracetamol, and saves it. Two days later when the boxes arrive, she opens the same order and clicks Receive — the new stock appears instantly, with its batch number and expiry.
End of month · tidying up Once a month Sara does a Stock control count: she walks the shelves, types in what she physically counted, and the page shows where reality differs from the system. She fixes the differences and approves the count. She also glances at Reports to see her best-selling drugs and what expired (wastage). If she ever needs to add a brand-new drug she never stocked before, she uses Catalog admin.

Sara's reality: every day she uses Dispense queue + Dispense detail and OTC sale. Dashboard and Purchasing are weekly. Batches, Stock control, Catalog admin and Reports are occasional. One person, all pages.

The big-pharmacy idea: the manager decides what we sell and what we buy; the dispensers serve patients; the stock-keeper guards the shelves; the cashier takes the money. Each role only sees its own pages.

⭐ The one golden rule — prescriptions are “pay first, collect after.”

A prescription is never handed over until it's paid. The flow is always: doctor signs → pharmacist prices it (Prepare) → patient pays the cashier → pharmacist hands over (Complete). This keeps every Birr of prescription revenue going through the cashier. (The only exception is an over-the-counter walk-in sale, which is paid right at the pharmacy counter.)

Every page, explained

For each page: what it's for, a quick real-world picture, and who uses it in a small vs large pharmacy.

1 Dispense queue

Your “to-do list” of prescriptions waiting to be filled.

The instant a doctor signs a prescription, it lands here by itself. You see the patient, the Rx number, how many items, and a flag if the patient has any allergy. It refreshes on its own. You don't do anything here — you just click the patient you want to serve next.

Picture it: Almaz's prescription pops onto the list as “R-1042 · 2 items · allergy check.” You click it.

Small: Sara watches it all dayLarge: the shared bench — several dispensers pull from it

2 Dispense detail (opens when you click a queue row)

The actual filling screen for one prescription.

Here you do the real work for each prescribed drug:

  1. Check the allergy warning — if the drug clashes with the patient's allergy, you'll see red. Call the doctor if needed.
  2. Pick the stock lot (the page suggests the soonest-to-expire one) and the quantity.
  3. Click Prepare → it prices the fill and sends the bill to the cashier.
  4. After the patient pays, click Complete → stock drops, you hand over the medicine.

If a prescribed drug isn't one your pharmacy stocks, the line says “Not in stock — print & buy outside,” and the patient buys it elsewhere.

Small: Sara, every prescriptionLarge: dispensers; the cashier handles the payment in between

3 OTC sale

Selling something over the counter — no doctor, no prescription.

For walk-ins buying things like paracetamol, bandages, or vitamins. Search the drug, build a cart, add a discount if you like, take payment, done. Stock drops immediately. Payment is taken right here at the counter.

Picture it: someone wants cough syrup. Search, add to cart, take 80 ETB, finished.

Small: Sara, for every walk-inLarge: the counter dispenser

4 Drug catalog

A read-only list of every drug you carry and how much is on hand.

Think of it as your shelf list. You can't change anything here — it's for looking things up: “Do we stock Metformin? How much is left? What's the price?” Low-stock items are highlighted.

Small: a quick look-upLarge: anyone on the floor checking availability

5 Batches & expiry

Tracking each physical lot of stock — its expiry and quantity — and handling problem stock.

Every delivery comes in “lots” (batches), each with its own expiry date. This page lists them all and flags ones that are near expiry or expired. Two actions live here: correct a quantity (if a count was wrong, or to log breakage) and quarantine a lot — lock it so it can't be sold (for a damaged box or a recall).

Picture it: Dawit finds a crushed box of syrup and quarantines that lot so no one dispenses it.

Small: occasional — fix counts, pull expired stockLarge: the stock-keeper's home page

6 Pharmacy dashboard

The manager's morning overview — the health of the pharmacy on one screen.

Numbers at a glance: how many filled today, total value of stock (ETB), how many drugs are low, how many lots are expiring soon, how many orders are open — plus a small alerts list and a chart of the last two weeks of dispensing.

Small: Sara's quick morning checkLarge: the manager, daily

7 Catalog admin

Adding and editing the drugs your pharmacy carries (and their prices).

This is where you define a drug: its name, form (tablet/syrup), price in ETB, the reorder level (the stock level that triggers a “low stock” warning), whether it's a controlled drug, and whether it's active. You set up a drug here once; you sell it many times.

Picture it: a new antibiotic arrives in your formulary — you add it here with its price and reorder level before stocking it.

Small: Sara, when adding a new productLarge: the manager only

8 Purchasing

Buying stock from suppliers — and receiving it.

Three steps: keep a list of your suppliers; raise a purchase order (PO) listing what you want to buy; and when the delivery arrives, click Receive — which turns the order into real stock on the shelf (new batches with their expiry dates).

Picture it: stock is low, so you order 500 Amoxicillin caps. Two days later the box arrives — you Receive the order and the 500 appear in stock.

Small: Sara, weekly restockLarge: the manager raises orders; the stock-keeper confirms receipt

9 Stock control

Checking that the shelf matches the system — a physical count.

Reality drifts from records (miscounts, breakage, theft). Here you start a count, the system snapshots what it thinks you have, you type what you actually counted, and it shows the difference for each drug. You fix the differences, then approve the count to lock it.

Picture it: the system says 120 paracetamol but you count 110. You record 110; the system corrects itself and logs the 10 missing.

Small: Sara, monthlyLarge: the stock-keeper, on a regular cycle

10 Reports

The numbers for decisions — six ready-made reports you can view or export.

  • Consumption — what's selling most
  • Margins — sell price vs cost
  • Wastage — expired / quarantined stock you lost
  • Controlled drugs — register of controlled-substance movements
  • Stock valuation — what your inventory is worth
  • Supplier — supplier performance

Known limitations & improvement ideas

An honest heads-up about rough edges you'll meet during setup and daily use, with what I'd improve. None of these stop you running the pharmacy today — they're things to plan around, and a wish-list for later.

🔴 important · 🟠 worth knowing · 🟡 minor

Setting up & your drug list

🟠 No bulk import. Every drug and every opening-stock batch is entered by hand — no spreadsheet/CSV upload — so a 150–300 line formulary is a long, error-prone setup. Improve: CSV/Excel import for the catalog and for opening stock.
🔴 The pharmacy catalog and the doctors' prescribing list are two separate lists. They're linked only by exact (case-insensitive) drug name. A spelling, brand, or strength mismatch means a prescription line can't be dispensed and tells the patient to “buy outside” — even when you have it in stock. Improve: link the two by a shared drug ID, or add synonym/fuzzy matching and a “map this prescription to a stock item” step.
🟡 Thin catalog housekeeping. Drugs can't be deleted (only set inactive); “form” and “class” are free text with no standard list (inconsistent wording weakens the allergy check and report grouping); price and reorder level aren't validated (negatives are accepted). Improve: standard form/class lists, basic number validation, and a proper soft-delete.

Stock & inventory

🟠 Purchase-order cost defaults to the selling price. Each PO line pre-fills cost with the sell price, so unless you overwrite it your cost, margin, and valuation numbers are wrong. Improve: default to last cost or leave blank — never the sell price.
🟠 Inventory is valued at selling price, not cost. The Dashboard “value on hand” and the valuation report use sell price and include quarantined/expired lots, so they overstate what your stock is really worth. Improve: cost-based valuation that excludes quarantined/expired stock.
🟠 Receiving a delivery is all-or-nothing. The whole order is received at the full ordered quantity, in a single batch — no partial shipments, no “received 80 of 100,” no splitting one line across expiry dates. Improve: line-level partial receipt with a received quantity and multiple batches.
🟠 Stock counts correct the largest lot. A count adjustment is applied to your single largest batch (or invents a never-expiring “COUNT-ADJ” lot), so batch/expiry accuracy degrades after a count. Improve: enter counted quantities per batch.
🟡 The movement log and on-hand quantity aren't reconciled. On-hand is the batch quantity; the stock-movement history is a separate audit trail that's never summed back, so the two can drift with nothing checking. Improve: a periodic reconcile (or derive on-hand from the ledger).

Dispensing & selling

🔴 No partial fills. If you stock only some of a prescription's items, completing it marks the whole prescription dispensed — there's no record of which lines were actually filled versus sent outside. Improve: per-line dispense status (filled / partial / referred out).
🟠 A priced-but-unpaid prescription can't be cancelled. If the patient declines at the cashier after you've prepared it, there's no “void” button — it stays stuck “awaiting payment” and you can't re-prepare it. Improve: add a cancel/void action for an awaiting-payment dispense.
🟠 Allergy checking is basic. It's a simple name/word match, not a real interaction database; very short drug names slip through, and OTC sales aren't allergy-checked at all. Improve: proper allergy/interaction checking, including on OTC.
🟠 OTC cash bypasses the cashier. Walk-in OTC sales record themselves as paid at the pharmacy counter and don't flow through the central cashier/finance the way prescriptions do. Improve: route OTC through the same billing spine, or add a daily cash reconciliation.

Reporting & day-to-day

🟠 Reports have no date range and no totals. Every report runs over all history with no period filter or total row, and some are capped — the controlled-drugs register shows only the most recent 100 movements, so it isn't a complete regulatory export. Improve: date filters, total rows, and a full controlled-drug export.
🟡 “Today” counts use UTC, not local time. The day rolls over around 3 AM Addis time, so late-night/early-morning activity can land on the wrong day. Improve: use the clinic's local day boundary.
🟡 No proper labels or receipts. The dispensing “print” prints the screen rather than a medicine label, and OTC has no printed receipt or change-due calculation. Improve: medicine-label and receipt templates.

That's the whole pharmacy. If you remember just one thing: prescriptions are pay-first (queue → prepare → cashier → complete), and walk-in OTC is pay-at-the-counter. Everything else — catalog, purchasing, batches, counts, reports — exists to keep those two counters stocked and honest.
