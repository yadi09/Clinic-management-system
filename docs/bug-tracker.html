1 Critical All backups sit on the same server as the database OPS-1 · Reliability · effort S Verified

2 Critical Removed staff keep access for up to a month SEC-2 · Safety & access · effort S Verified
What happens
When the owner deactivates or suspends a staff member, only new sign-ins are blocked. A phone or computer that was already signed in keeps renewing itself quietly and keeps full access for up to 30 days. Worse, an old password-reset link they still hold switches their account back on.

Why it matters
Someone the clinic has deliberately locked out can still read patient records and take actions in the clinic's name.
The fix
When a staff member is deactivated or suspended, sign them out on every device at once and cancel their open invite and reset links; the renewal step will also check the account is still active.

4 Critical A file record can point at another clinic's stored file SEC-1 · Safety & access · effort S Verified
What happens
After a file is uploaded, the app tells the server where the file is stored, and the server believes it. It does not check that the location belongs to this clinic and this patient, or that the chosen visit belongs to this patient. Opening the record then gives a link to that file, and deleting it deletes that file.
In the clinic
A staff member at Clinic A who somehow learns the storage location of an X-ray at Clinic B records it on one of her own patients. She can now view Clinic B's X-ray, and pressing Delete erases it from Clinic B too. In the same way an X-ray can be filed on a different patient's visit or on the down payment.
Why it matters
Another clinic's patient files could be read or destroyed. The locations contain long random codes, which makes this hard to exploit, but it should not depend on that.
The fix
Only accept a storage location that sits in this clinic's own folder for this patient (or have the server hand out the location itself), and check that the chosen visit belongs to the same patient.
Who notices
Every clinic's X-rays and attachments

5 Critical Allergy written as a plural does not warn on prescriptions NEW-KERNEL-ALLERGY-1 · Safety & access · effort S Verified
What happens
When a prescription is written, the system compares the drug name with the patient's recorded allergies. It only warns if the allergy word appears whole inside the drug name. So an allergy recorded as "Penicillins" (plural) does not warn for "Penicillin V", because "penicillins" is not inside "penicillin v". The same happens for other longer spellings, and the check also does not know drug families (a "Penicillin" allergy only catches Amoxicillin if the clinic's drug list has tagged Amoxicillin as a penicillin).
In the clinic
Reception records Almaz's allergy as "Penicillins – severe". Two weeks later Dr Selam prescribes Penicillin V 250 mg for an abscess. No warning appears, the prescription prints, and the only protection left is whether Dr Selam happens to look at the allergy line on the card.
Why it matters
A drug the patient is known to react to can be prescribed with no warning, while staff trust that the system would have stopped it. That is a direct patient-harm risk.
The fix
Make the match work both ways and ignore simple plural endings (Penicillins = Penicillin), and warn on drug families using the drug list's allergen tags. Add a short list of common families (penicillins, sulfa, NSAIDs) so they are caught even when a drug was typed freely.
Who notices
Dentist, Doctor, Patient
